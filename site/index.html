About this website

How this was made

Every week or two, a volunteer at the Australian National Botanic Gardens in Canberra walks through the Gardens and writes a short leaflet called In Flower This Week. It describes the plants in bloom along that day's walk. The Gardens have kept these leaflets online, from April 1997 to August 2016.

This website uses those leaflets to answer a simple question: are the plants in the Gardens flowering earlier than they used to, and if so, why?

What was done, step by step

  1. Every leaflet was collected

    All 899 leaflets were gathered. Most are still on the Gardens' website. 52 had gone missing, and copies of those were found in the Internet Archive, a public library of old web pages.

  2. The plants in each leaflet were picked out

    In every leaflet, the plants in flower are printed in bold. A computer program read each leaflet and wrote down every bold plant name, together with the leaflet's date. This gave 18,620 records of a plant in flower on a particular day.

  3. The plant names were tidied up

    Botanists sometimes rename plants, and a few names in the leaflets were misspelt. Each name was checked against the Atlas of Living Australia, the national list of Australian species. That way the same plant is counted as one plant across all twenty years, even if its name changed.

  4. Each plant's flowering date was tracked over the years

    For every plant, the first leaflet each year that mentioned it was noted. Only plants that appeared in at least eight different years were used, which left 326 plants. Then came the key question: did that first date move earlier or later as the years went by?

  5. The results were compared with Canberra's weather

    Official temperature records for Canberra from the Bureau of Meteorology, and rainfall records, were used to see whether plants flowered earlier in warmer or wetter years.

  6. The work was double-checked

    From 2014 the leaflets came out every two weeks instead of every week, and over time they began to list more plants. Changes like these could make plants look earlier or later when nothing had really changed. So the analysis was repeated in several ways that remove these effects, and the result held each time. A separate page also lets anyone compare a leaflet with what the computer picked out, to measure how accurate it was.

What was found

Plants are appearing in the leaflets earlier than they used to. On average, they now show up about 5 to 7 days earlier for every ten years that pass. Over the twenty years of leaflets, that adds up to roughly one to two weeks earlier.

Canberra has warmed, and warmth plays a part. Winters and springs in Canberra were about half a degree warmer each decade over this period. After a warm winter, plants did tend to flower early.

But warming doesn't seem to explain all of it. The warming was fairly small, and on its own it would account for only part of the change. Other likely reasons include changes in the Gardens themselves, such as plants maturing, new plantings and watering, and changes in which volunteers wrote the leaflets and which paths they walked.

Things to keep in mind

  • The leaflets were written to guide visitors, not as scientific records. A plant only appears when a volunteer chose to walk past it and mention it.
  • Because of this, the result for any single plant is not reliable. The overall pattern across hundreds of plants is much more trustworthy.
  • The findings are best read as a strong hint, not as proof.

The garden-wide signal

Flowering onset, all taxa combined

For each species, every season's first appearance in the brochures is compared with that species' own average. Averaging those differences across all species in a year gives a garden-wide onset index. Below zero means the garden flowered earlier than usual.

Species by species

Which flowers are shifting

326 taxa appear in at least 8 well-sampled seasons spanning 10+ years. The shift is a linear trend in first-appearance date, in days per decade. The table opens sorted by strength of evidence (p). Long-flowering taxa are hidden by default: when a plant is in flower most of the year, its "first appearance" depends on which week a volunteer walked past, so its trend is unreliable. Individual species' records are noisy, because a plant only shows up if the volunteer's walk that week passed it. So read single-species results as leads, and check the q value (false discovery rate across all 326 tests) before trusting one.
