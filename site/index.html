About this website

How this was made

Every week or two, a volunteer at the Australian National Botanic Gardens in Canberra walks through the Gardens and writes a short leaflet describing the plants in bloom along that day's walk. From 1997 to 2016 the Gardens published it as In Flower This Week. Since August 2016 the Friends of the Gardens have carried it on as Flowers, Fruit & Foliage. Together the two series cover nearly thirty years.

This website uses those leaflets to answer a simple question: are the plants in the Gardens flowering earlier than they used to, and if so, why?

What was done, step by step

  1. Every leaflet was collected

    899 leaflets from the Gardens (1997 to 2016) and 248 from the Friends (2016 to today) were gathered. Most are still online. About 180 had gone missing, including many of the Friends' older leaflets after their old website closed. Copies of those were found in the Internet Archive, a public library of old web pages.

  2. The plants in each leaflet were picked out

    In every leaflet, the plants being featured are printed in bold. A computer program read each leaflet and wrote down every bold plant name, together with the leaflet's date. The Gardens' leaflets are web pages. The Friends' leaflets are printed sheets saved as PDFs, laid out in two columns with numbered stops, and a few store their text in a scrambled form that had to be decoded. This gave 22,000 records of a plant in flower on a particular day.

  3. The plant names were tidied up

    Botanists sometimes rename plants, and a few names in the leaflets were misspelt. Each name was checked against the Atlas of Living Australia, the national list of Australian species. That way the same plant is counted as one plant across all the years, even if its name changed.

  4. Each plant's flowering time was worked out for every year

    For every plant, the dates it appeared in the leaflets each year were noted, and the middle of those dates was taken as that year's flowering time. Only plants seen in at least eight different years were used, which left 436 plants. Then came the key question: did that flowering time move earlier or later as the years went by?

  5. The results were compared with Canberra's weather

    Official temperature records for Canberra from the Bureau of Meteorology, and rainfall records, were used to see whether plants flowered earlier in warmer or wetter years. The Bureau's long-term record currently ends in 2024. For the months since then, a weather model that closely matches the Bureau's figures was used.

  6. The work was double-checked

    In 2014 the leaflets changed format. They began coming out every two weeks instead of every week, and each became a fixed walk of 15 stops instead of about 20 plants. With fewer leaflets and fewer plants, a plant tends to be spotted a little later, even if nothing has changed. That's why the middle of each plant's flowering time is used rather than the first day it was seen. The analysis was also repeated in several other ways, for example treating the early years as if they too were fortnightly, and using the Gardens' leaflets on their own. The result held each time, and nothing changed when the Friends took over the leaflets in 2016. A separate page lets anyone compare a leaflet with what the computer picked out, to measure how accurate it was.

What was found

The plants are flowering earlier than they used to. On average, their flowering time has moved about 5 days earlier for every ten years. Over the whole period that adds up to roughly two weeks.

Warmth plays a part. Canberra has warmed by about a third of a degree each decade since 1997. In warmer years the plants flowered earlier, by around 3 to 7 days for each degree of extra warmth.

But warming explains only part of it. On these figures, the warming accounts for roughly a fifth to a third of the change. Rainfall made no clear difference. The rest is likely due to changes in the Gardens themselves, such as plants maturing, new plantings and watering, and to changes in who wrote the leaflets and which paths they walked.

Things to keep in mind

  • The leaflets were written to guide visitors, not as scientific records. A plant only appears when a volunteer chose to walk past it and mention it.
  • Because of this, the result for any single plant is not reliable. The overall pattern across hundreds of plants is much more trustworthy.
  • Since 2014 each leaflet features 15 plants and comes out every two weeks, so recent years have about a third as many records as the early years. The analysis allows for this, but it does make recent years less certain.
  • There are no leaflets for April to June 2020 or for mid-August to October 2021, the periods of COVID-19 restrictions and lockdown in Canberra.
  • The findings are best read as a strong hint, not as proof.

Every leaflet, and what was pulled out of it

Both series put the featured plants in bold. The ANBG web pages (1997–2016) usually follow each name with a garden bed reference like [Section 30]. The Friends' PDFs (2016–now) number each stop on the walk. The parser reads that convention, cleans the name, and resolves it against the Atlas of Living Australia so misspellings and renamed species (e.g. Bracteantha bracteata → Xerochrysum bracteatum) join up across the years. Findings

Which flowers are blooming earlier, and why?

The garden-wide signal

Flowering time, all taxa combined

Each taxon's mid-flowering date is the average date of its mentions in a season. For every season it is compared with that taxon's own long-run average. Averaging those differences across all taxa gives a garden-wide index for the year. Below zero means the garden flowered earlier than usual. The mid-flowering date is the main measure because it isn't thrown off by the switch from weekly to fortnightly leaflets in 2014. First appearance is shown as a check.

    Check accuracy

    Check the extraction against the originals

    This page draws a random sample of one leaflet per year, 1997 to today (30 in total). The sample covers both series. For each leaflet, compare the original on the left with what was extracted on the right. Mark every row, add any bold plants that were missed, then mark the leaflet done. The scores update as you go.

    What to look for. In the Gardens' web pages (1997–2016), the featured plants are in bold, usually followed by a bed number such as [Section 30]. In the Friends' PDF leaflets (2016 on), each numbered stop names its plant in bold italics. The stop numbers on the right match the numbers in the leaflet. The PDFs are in two columns, so read them by stop number rather than top to bottom. If an original doesn't appear (Internet Archive copies can be slow), use Open in a new tab.

    Your marks save in this browser only. When you finish, use Download results and send the file to Ross. Several people's files can be combined, and the scores then appear in the PDF report too.
